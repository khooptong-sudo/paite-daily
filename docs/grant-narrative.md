## 1. Summary

Paite Daily is a volunteer-reviewed corpus of Paite, a Tibeto-Burman language of Manipur with 78,725
speakers. It collects Paite–English sentence pairs from speakers, has every pair checked by a second
native speaker before publication, and releases the verified result to the public domain. In
⟨CURRENT: 14⟩ months it has grown to ⟨CURRENT: 521⟩ verified pairs with five volunteer reviewers, no
funding and no advertising.

The grant would pay reviewers for the hours they currently donate, add a recorded audio component,
and put the corpus somewhere permanent with a DOI. The point is not to keep a website alive. It is to
produce the aligned text that a Paite spellchecker, keyboard dictionary or translation model would
have to be built from, text that does not currently exist in any form.

## 2. The language

Paite (ISO 639-3: `pck`) is an independent Tibeto-Burman language, spoken mainly in Churachandpur
district of Manipur, with communities in Mizoram and Assam and among Paite families in Myanmar. The
2011 Census of India records 78,725 people whose mother tongue is Paite. It is a non-scheduled
language, taught as a modern Indian language subject up to the three-year degree course at Manipur
University since 2004, and broadcast daily on All India Radio Imphal.

The community's relationship with print is old and strong. Christianity arrived in the nineteenth
century; literacy followed early and held. Paite has its own alphabet, the Paite Laimal, devised by
T. Vialphung in 1903 and published in *Sintung Bu* in 1945. It has a dictionary tradition reaching
back a century, most recently the Paite Tribe Council's *English–Paite Dictionary* in three editions
(1993, 2003, 2014), and one book-length reference grammar, Naorem Saratchandra Singh's *A Grammar of
Paite* (Mittal, 2006). Proverbs are documented in T. Thuamkhopau's *Paite paunaak leh pau upate*
(2009).

## 3. The gap

Everything about Paite is written down except the thing that matters now.

A Paite speaker can write a sentence by hand and cannot write it on a phone. There is no
spellchecker, no predictive keyboard, no parallel text, no concordance and no machine translation,
because every one of those is built from aligned, machine-readable sentences, and for Paite those
sentences have never been assembled. A dictionary tells you what a word means. It does not tell a
computer how the language behaves in a line of text. Only a corpus does, and Paite has none.

This is the ordinary condition of a small language in 2026: not unwritten, but unmachine-readable.
The consequence is that Paite speakers type in Manipuri or English and cannot use their own language
in the place where writing now mostly happens.

## 4. What already exists

- **⟨CURRENT: 521⟩ verified Paite–English pairs**, each written by one speaker and approved by
  another before publication. Unreviewed submissions are never shown as answers.
- **Five volunteer reviewers.** All native speakers, all named in the corpus metadata.
- **A published public-domain commitment.** Every verified pair is released CC0, irrevocably, with
  no attribution requirement, so that a keyboard project, a school, or a translation team can use
  it without asking us.
- **Open data already published.** The wider corpus is public at
  `github.com/khooptong-sudo/paite-open-language-data`, the Paite–English dictionary, verse pairs,
  hymns and native-speaker corrections, so this project is not proposing to become open. It already
  is, and has been releasing without a grant.
- **A documented review protocol.** Contributors tag each pair *Gen Thoh* (spoken), *Gelh Thoh*
  (written) or *Anih In* (both), so register is recorded, not lost.
- **Abuse protection.** Submissions are gated against automated abuse; publisher identity is not
  asserted for any pair that has not passed a human reviewer.

## 5. What the grant pays for

**Activity 1, paid review hours.** Five reviewers, twenty hours each, at US$10/hour. The work is
what they already do unpaid: read a submitted pair against its source, decide whether it is correct
Paite, correct the orthography against the Paite Laimal, and mark it. Paid hours convert a hobby
rhythm into a schedule, and they make it possible to ask a reviewer for twenty considered hours
rather than an unlimited favour.

**Activity 2, orthography and consistency pass.** Forty hours at US$10/hour, on the whole existing
corpus: one reviewer working through all pairs for consistency of spelling, tone marking and
register labelling. A corpus with three spellings of the same word is worse than no corpus, because
the error propagates into every model trained on it.

**Activity 3, audio.** One hundred proverb pairs recorded with two native speakers, with the audio,
transcription and English gloss stored as one unit. Proverbs are where the language is densest and
where text alone loses the most; and *pau upa* is the part of the site speakers ask about first.

**Activity 4, release and deposit.** Export in a documented open format (JSONL plus TSV), a data
statement describing collection, review and licensing, and deposit with a DOI so the corpus is
citable and survives the website.

## 6. Method and governance

A pair is published only after a second native speaker has checked it. That is the entire quality
mechanism, and it is sufficient because it is the same mechanism the community uses for its
dictionaries. Submissions are gated against automated abuse; unreviewed material is never displayed
as an answer; no machine-generated text enters the corpus, and no model decides what counts as
correct Paite. Where a machine is used at all, it is used to *organise* what humans have already
verified, never to assert a fact about the language.

Recording register matters as much as recording content. Paite has formal and intimate styles, and
a corpus that mixes them without labels teaches a model to mix them too. Hence the *Gen Thoh* /
*Gelh Thoh* / *Anih In* tag on every pair.

## 7. Openness, and what happens if we stop

The corpus is CC0 and the site is a static export. Anyone can fork it, rehost it for the price of a
domain, or take the data and build something we have not thought of. This is deliberate: a corpus
that requires permission is a corpus that gets ignored, and a corpus that gets ignored dies with its
website. If the project ends, the deposited data does not.

The project takes no advertising and will not. Advertising against a 78,725-speaker language is
worth a few dollars a month and would be paid for by the reader's attention rather than by anyone
who cares about the language.

## Appendix A, classification, for forms that demand it

Where a form requires a language family, write **Tibeto-Burman (Sino-Tibetan)** and the code
**`pck`**. Do not write "Kuki-Chin" or "Chin": these are exonyms, and Paite is an independent
language, not a variety of any neighbouring one. If a reviewer asks why the literature indexes it
differently, the answer is one sentence: *the older literature classified Paite within a broader
colonial grouping; the community's own name for the language, and the ISO code, are what this
project uses.*

## Appendix B, what not to claim

- Do not describe Paite as "endangered" in the sense of losing its home speakers. It is not. The
  claim being made is about digital absence, which is true, checkable, and sufficient.
- Do not use an ethnic-population figure in place of the mother-tongue figure. 78,725 is the 2011
  mother-tongue count and is the only number this project should cite.
- Do not present budgeted stipend hours as past hours worked.
